Chapter one

A welcome for every child

Walking into club should feel like being expected. A child’s own name on the big screen says exactly that.

The lobby TV is the first thing a family sees. Every check-in becomes a short, joyful moment on it, and none of it needs a volunteer standing by the screen.

Small moments, every single week

Birthdays, first visits, a child back for a new season: each gets its own words above and below the name, and a birthday or a very first visit gets the one bright sticker, so the room learns to cheer along.

The celebrations are for everyone in the lobby, and the screen still never learns a last name, an age or anything about a family.

  • Birthday weeks
  • First-time welcomes
  • Welcome back
  • Milestone cheers

  • Their name, on their club’s wave

    Their club’s wave rises from the bottom of the screen and carries their first name, in the club’s own colors. Each name also flies in its own way, the same every week, so a child comes to recognize their entrance.

    • Puggles
    • Cubbies
    • Sparks
    • T&T
    • Trek
    • Journey
    Peek under the hood: Their name, on their club’s wave
    The colors are the 2026-27 club palette, kept in one shared kit that every screen in the family reads. The entrance (one of three ways the letters arrive) is seeded from the name itself, so it is the same on every screen, every week. Each name is measured to fit on one line and never breaks between letters. Common spellings such as “Truth and Training” or “TnT” land on the right club, and an unknown club still gets a cheerful warm-orange wave.

  • Birthdays get the bright sticker

    During a child’s birthday week the words above their name say “Happy birthday,” a bright starburst sticker lands beside it, and confetti in warm colors and their club’s own pops out of the sticker. No age is ever shown.

    Peek under the hood: Birthdays get the bright sticker
    The check-in feed carries a yes-or-no birthday flag and never a birth year, so the sticker says “Happy birthday!” and never a number. When the birthday falls later in the week, a “Birthday this Friday!” style line replaces “it’s your special day,” which would be wrong on a Wednesday. Once the church sets its display key, that week’s birthday list travels sealed, so only screens holding the key can show that line.

  • First night, and welcome back

    A child’s very first night says “Welcome to Awana Clubs” above their name and gets a bright “New!” sticker. A child returning for a new season is greeted “Welcome back” instead.

    Welcome to Awana ClubsNORA

    New!
    Peek under the hood: First night, and welcome back
    Both come from the check-in system, which marks a first visit and a first night of the season separately and never sends both for the same child. Welcome back is deliberately quieter, with no sticker: the one bright sticker is kept for what is new or special, so it keeps meaning something.

  • Never a pile-up at the door

    When a whole carpool arrives at once, every child still gets their full moment. The wave stays up and the next name flips in, like a scoreboard, and a “+3 more coming” note lets the room know.

    EliGraceLeo+3 more coming
    Peek under the hood: Never a pile-up at the door
    Nobody’s time is ever shortened. Each child holds the screen for the full time set in Settings (6 seconds, or 8 for a birthday or a first visit, by default), however long the line. Arrivals wait their turn in order, and when the next child is from another club, that club’s wave sweeps in over the last one.

  • Big numbers get a cheer

    The 50th, the 100th, the 200th child of the evening each bring a room-wide moment, and so do handbooks finished and awards earned tonight.

    Triple digits100 kids tonight!
    Peek under the hood: Big numbers get a cheer
    The big ones (every 50th, from the 50th up to the 300th) come from the check-in desk’s own total, not from each screen’s count, so every screen in the building cheers at the same moment. Each is celebrated once, in order, even when the count jumps past it. Each screen also cheers every 25th check-in it sees, by its own count.

  • Quieter once the program starts

    Children who arrive once the opening has begun still get their welcome, just a calmer one: no confetti, and the chime turned down, so the room isn’t pulled away from what’s happening.

    Peek under the hood: Quieter once the program starts
    The display reads the same shared schedule file as the projector, so it knows when the program has begun. The chime itself is off until a church turns it on in Settings.

Chapter two

The projector, run for you

The best club nights keep moving. The projector keeps time, so the leaders can keep the kids.

A second page of the same app runs on the main-room projector. It counts down all week, opens the night, times each club’s game slot and signs off, all from the church’s posted schedule. Nobody has to click through slides during the meeting.

  • A countdown all week long

    Between club nights the projector counts down to the next meeting in big, glowing digits, with the day’s weather playing quietly behind it and upcoming church events listed underneath.

    Peek under the hood: A countdown all week long
    The countdown comes from one small schedule engine reading shared/schedule.json, the same file the lobby screen follows. Its tests check every hand-off of the evening, and every deploy replays those moments in a real browser before it goes live.

  • The opening, on cue

    At start time the welcome slide and the pledges come up on their own, then the screen hands the room to the first game slot. At the end of the night it closes and signs off.

    Peek under the hood: The opening, on cue
    Each part of the evening (opening, each club’s game slot, closing, shutdown) is one line in the schedule file, so retiming the night is an edit to one file, not to the app.

  • Game time for each club

    Each game slot gets its own screen in its club’s colors, with a big timer counting to the next handoff and a quiet count of how many of its kids have checked in tonight.

    1. T&T6:05
    2. Sparks6:30
    3. Puggles & Cubbies7:00
    Peek under the hood: Game time for each club
    The per-club count arrives through the same checked, first-names-only connection the lobby uses; the projector never opens a second one. If the check-in desk goes quiet for ten minutes, the count hides rather than showing a stale number.

  • A calm heads-up

    Two minutes before a handoff the timer turns amber, and red for the last thirty seconds. No pop-ups, just a color a leader can read across the gym.

    1:470:24two minutes, then the final call
    Chime off by default
    Peek under the hood: A calm heads-up
    An optional chime can be armed from the projector’s hidden menu. Until someone does, the projector never makes a sound on its own.

Chapter three

Runs itself

The volunteers who set this up won’t always be in the room. So it was built to look after itself, night after night.

New screens log themselves in, announcements reach every display at once and take turns with the welcomes, updates install on their own, and a quiet night never looks like a broken one.

Set up once, then left alone

Most of the careful work here is making sure nobody has to babysit a screen. A church sets it up before the first family walks in, and after that it takes care of itself.

  • One passphrase
  • Publish once
  • Updates itself
  • Rides out Wi-Fi drops

Announcements and welcomes take turns

A slide the room needs to read can be marked “Hold check-ins.” While it is up, arriving names wait behind a small “Waiting” count instead of covering it, then each child gets their full welcome on the next slide. The fall event promos always hold the line this way.

It works the other way too. While names are on screen the slideshow pauses, keeping the time a slide has already had, so a rush never skips or restarts one. A sweeping wave carries the lobby into and out of each held slide.

Opt-in, slide by slide

  • One passphrase logs in every screen

    A new or replacement TV needs only the church’s passphrase, typed once. The screen receives its keys and starts following whatever has been published.

    Opt-in
    Peek under the hood: One passphrase logs in every screen
    The passphrase derives a key (PBKDF2-SHA256, 600,000 rounds) that opens a sealed setup message from the printer app, which must be running while a screen logs in. The keys it delivers live apart from Settings, so they never leave in a settings export.

  • One slide deck, every screen

    Type an announcement once at the check-in computer and press Publish. Every lobby display shows it, and a screen that was switched off catches up within a few minutes of coming back, as long as the printer app is running.

    Set up once at the check-in computer

    Peek under the hood: One slide deck, every screen
    Once the church sets its display key, the deck travels sealed and reaches every screen that holds it. It arrives in ordered pieces, and each screen keeps the last one it received, so a reboot on club night shows it at once. A slide’s “Hold check-ins” mark travels with it (from Club Label Printer 6.16). Only typed slides travel; uploaded videos stay on their own device. Any screen can opt out and keep its own slides.

  • Updates arrive on their own

    New versions install themselves within a few minutes, but never in the middle of a welcome, a celebration, or while someone is typing in Settings.

    1. New version found
    2. Waiting for Ava’s welcome to finish
    3. Updated while the room was quiet
    Peek under the hood: Updates arrive on their own
    Every 3 minutes a screen asks which build is current. It reloads only once the new page is really being served (so it can’t loop on an old copy), and only when no welcome, celebration, pick-up board or open panel is on screen and nobody is typing. A busy screen asks again every 15 seconds, for as long as it takes; there is no deadline that could interrupt a check-in rush.

  • Steady through a shaky connection

    If the Wi-Fi drops, the screen keeps its clock, slides and schedule. When it reconnects, children who arrived during the gap get a quieter “Also joined us tonight” welcome.

    Peek under the hood: Steady through a shaky connection
    A small offline cache keeps the page itself available. The check-in desk regularly re-sends recent arrivals; the screen replays the ones from the last 20 minutes it hasn’t already shown, matched one by one, so nobody is greeted twice.

  • Honest when something is off

    When Club Label Printer runs a practice night, the screen wears a rehearsal watermark, so a test check-in is never mistaken for a real arrival. If names stop arriving, the screen says why in plain words instead of going silently still.

    rehearsal — practice run, not real check-ins
    Counts rising, no names — check the printer
    Peek under the hood: Honest when something is off
    Counts and status messages travel unsealed on purpose. That is what lets a screen tell “the connection is down” from “I can’t read names” from “it’s just a quiet night.” A problem sticker shows whenever there is one, whatever slide is up. A test welcome fired from Settings wears a demo badge and never changes the evening’s count.

  • Gentle on the smallest screen

    Most of the day, Journey Display’s kiosk shows this display on a small Raspberry Pi computer. It opens it in a low-power mode with no confetti and no animation at all, so it stays smooth. Every other screen keeps its full celebrations.

    Peek under the hood: Gentle on the smallest screen
    ?lowPower=1 in that one embed’s address turns confetti off and freezes every animation, whatever the device’s saved settings say. Every animated part of the display goes through one shared helper, and every animation ends on its finished frame, so the frozen screen still shows the whole welcome, and anything added later is covered too.

Chapter four

Safe by design

A screen in a room full of children has to be careful with what it knows. This one is built to know very little.

It starts from the assumption that anything arriving could be watched by a stranger. So it checks every message before showing it, and once the church sets its display key, children’s names travel sealed.

The promises behind every screen

  • First names only, through a strict allowlistEach kind of message is cut down to its approved fields before anything else sees it. Last names, allergies, contact details, birth years and photos can’t get through, even if they are sent.
  • Names are sealed on the wayOnce the church sets its display key, check-ins, birthdays, the pick-up list, the lobby slide deck and the screens’ shared settings are encrypted before they leave the check-in computer.
  • No quiet downgradeA screen that holds the key refuses an unsealed copy of those messages, so the protection can’t be switched off from outside.
  • The key stays out of settings filesIt lives in its own storage, so it is never included in a settings export and can’t be slipped in through a shared settings file.
  • No age on screenA birthday arrives as a yes-or-no flag, and this week’s birthday list carries only the month and day. No birth year ever reaches the display.
  • Checked before every updateEach change must pass lint, a type check, the tests, a build and a browser smoke test before it can reach a screen. The tests include messages stuffed with extra personal details, which must come out clean, and every pick-up board rule, case by case. If one fails, the working version stays live.

  • Plain about the limits

    Encryption hides who arrived, not that someone did. Someone with the public connection details could still see when messages are sent and how many, and the project’s security notes say so plainly.

    Peek under the hood: Plain about the limits
    Sealed messages use AES-256-GCM, with every check-in padded to the same length so a message’s size can’t hint at a name, and the screen and the printer app are pinned to one shared test file so the two sides can’t drift apart. Channel and event names stay readable so the service can deliver them. Counts, the schedule and notices are unsealed on purpose, since none of them names a child. There is no forward secrecy, so the notes advise changing the key between program years.

The pick-up board

A list that protects the children on it

At pickup time a strip along the bottom of the lobby screen lists which children haven’t been checked out yet, each club’s names after its own plate, so a parent or volunteer can see at a glance whose pickup hasn’t been recorded. The slides and announcements above it carry on as normal.

It comes on by itself from 7:30 pm on club nights (which clubs are on the list is set on the check-in laptop’s dashboard), says “Ava has checked out” there for a few seconds as each child leaves the list, and says so once everyone has been checked out. While it lists names, the corner counter can count down as children are checked out. It is most careful at exactly the moment that matters: when only a few children are left.

How the pick-up board is kept safe

  • Only at pickup timeIt comes on by itself from 7:30 pm on club nights, never while children are arriving, and the check-in laptop’s dashboard decides which clubs are on it. With no pick-up information yet, it shows nothing at all.
  • Stops naming anyone when only a few are leftAt three children or fewer by default, it names no one, gives no number (the corner counter stops counting too), says “a child has checked out” rather than a name, and points people to the check-in desk. Two names late in the evening would single out two specific children.
  • First names only, alwaysA first name under each club, and nothing else. No last names, allergies or contact details ever reach a screen.
  • “Not checked out yet,” never “still in the building”It shows what volunteers recorded at the desk, not a headcount, and every line says so. If the list stops updating, the board says that too.

Smaller things worth knowing

  • One thing in the corner at a time: the time, tonight’s count or the weather (from a free service, refreshed every 15 minutes, no account needed), changing only when a new slide loads and stepping aside for a slide that holds check-ins
  • A “Doors are open” moment for the first child checked in each night
  • Seasonal looks such as autumn, Christmas, snow day, Easter and VBS, picked by hand or chosen from the church calendar
  • Animated promos for the church’s own fall events, 15 seconds each and ending on a finished poster, counting down the club nights to each one and retiring themselves the morning after
  • The church’s own PowerPoint, uploaded and played right on the screen, or a looping video behind the welcomes
  • Simplified mode: one keyboard shortcut strips the screen to its reliable core mid-club, and welcomes keep working
  • Overlay mode for streaming software: welcomes and confetti over a live video feed
  • A club-color wash across the whole screen during a welcome, off by default
  • A chime with each welcome, off until a church turns it on
  • Keeps the TV awake during club, and a double-click makes it full screen
  • The projector hands itself back: after a leader jumps to another screen, it returns to the schedule unless they tap Stay
  • A low-power switch on the projector for an older laptop, off unless chosen or the computer asks for reduced motion
  • Holiday weeks can be marked as no club right on the projector
  • A running tally of books finished, awards earned and friends brought tonight, from the check-in desk’s own report
